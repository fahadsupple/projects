## Getting a Control on Both Sides of the Door

The turn knob on the inside of your door is a snib: twist it and the bolt goes across. It only exists on one face, so it is no use to you out on the landing. Older apartment entries were often built that way.

A smart lock is a two-part fitting, which is what makes it the answer here. The keypad, reader or fingerprint pad goes on the outside face; the motor and batteries go on the inside. The halves join through the door, which is why thickness matters so much: every model is built for a range, and a door outside it needs a different model.

Three things get read before anything is fitted: what is cut into the door now, what the leaf is made of, and how thick it is. A timber leaf and a metal-framed one call for different preparation. Where nothing has ever passed through the door, the outside face is marked out and cut on site, which adds time to the visit and is ordinary work.

Then it is commissioned with you: codes in, fingerprints enrolled, the app paired where the model uses one. You work the door yourself, from both sides, before anyone leaves.

## When Someone Else Needs to Get In

You may not be the person using this door every day. Perhaps you are setting up a parent's flat, or you are a carer coming and going on a roster. Either way the practical question is the same: how does the door open for you, without a key being cut and passed around?

Codes answer it. Each person gets their own, so the cleaner's code is not the carer's, and one can be withdrawn without touching the rest. On models that log it, you can see who came and went. An app, where the model has one and the network reaches the door, lets you open it without being there. A good many of these apartment jobs are arranged by a family member for that reason.

What a smart lock settles is entry. It is not a way of keeping anyone in: somebody who lives there can still open their own front door and walk out, and no lock we would fit changes that. If that is what sits behind the job, say so when you enquire, so nobody expects the hardware to do something it cannot.

## Deadbolt, Mortice or a Fire-Rated Unit Door Lock

On most doors the hardware already in place decides the format. A deadbolt sits in a round bore drilled through the face, and a smart deadbolt goes back into it. A mortice lock sits in a pocket routed into the door edge, and a smart mortice lock takes that over. A turn knob on a latch tells you less than either, because it can sit on a door prepared for either, or for neither.

So the format gets chosen for your door rather than inherited from it. If your question is which smart lock is the best one, the honest answer starts there: the shortlist is made at the door, not from a product page.

Unit entries carry one more filter. Where the entry door is fire-rated, the lock has to be a version built and tested for one. If the building's paperwork says so, or the door edge carries a tag, mention it early.

Most of the mechanical hardware on Melbourne entries is Lockwood, Lane or Lemaar, and a retrofit has to mate with whichever is on your door.
