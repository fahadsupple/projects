## What Happens to the Master Key When the Entrance Changes

A master key system is a hierarchy. Each flat has a key that opens its own door and the shared entrance, and above them sits one key that opens everything. Someone keeps a record of which key went to whom.

Put an electronic lock on that entrance and a single question decides how much of the arrangement survives: does the new lock keep a mechanical cylinder?

- With a cylinder, it can be keyed into the system already running. Existing resident keys go on opening the entrance, nothing has to be reissued, and the keypad or reader is an addition rather than a replacement.
- Without one, the entrance comes off the key system and everyone gets in on a code, a fob or the app instead. That works, provided it is chosen deliberately: every household then needs setting up, and somebody has to hold that list.

The cylinder is decided at purchase rather than at installation. A lock built without one cannot have one added afterwards.

If the block runs restricted keys, say so at the enquiry. Rekeying and key systems are standard locksmith work, so the entrance can be brought back onto the building's keying in the same visit instead of being left as an island.

## Handing Over Access, and Taking It Back

If you are turning a tenancy over, or letting a flat out for short stays, the entrance is the door that keeps generating work. The lock goes on once. Updating who can come through it happens for years afterwards.

Where the entrance runs on metal keys, keeping the register accurate after somebody moves out means cutting and issuing more of them. Where it runs on codes or fobs, the credential that has finished is removed and nothing else on the door is touched. A short stay can have a code that stops at checkout. A cleaner or a tradesperson can have one for the week.

None of that disturbs the master key system, provided the cylinder stayed in the door.

## Whether the Entrance Door Will Take the Lock You Want

The hardware already in an entrance door sets the direction. A round bore through the face means a deadbolt, and a smart deadbolt can take its place if the diameter, the backset and the door thickness agree with the model. A pocket routed into the door edge means a mortice lock, and a smart mortice lock takes that pocket over. Whatever is on it now, commonly a Lockwood, a Lane or a Lemaar, the new lock has to mate with the opening that hardware left behind.

Material counts as much as shape. Timber and metal-framed leaves call for different preparation, and an entrance never drilled for a deadbolt has to be set out and cut at the door. That makes the visit longer. It does not make it more complicated.

Then it is commissioned with you: codes loaded, fingerprints enrolled if the model reads them, and the app connected if there is one. The last thing done before anyone packs up is working the door from outside, shut, the way the building uses it every day.

If nothing has been ordered yet, four checks matter most on an entrance a whole block uses:

- Whether the model comes with a mechanical key cylinder, and whether that cylinder can be keyed to a system. That one decides whether existing keys keep working.

- The door thickness range in the specifications, measured against the actual leaf.
- Whether the outside face has room for the model's plate alongside whatever is already there, such as a pull handle or a door closer arm.
